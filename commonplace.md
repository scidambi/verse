# 2026-09-01 · Lyudmyla Khersonska, "When Crimea was still a place of light,"
steal: "and gulls were the only patrols in the sky"
cut: "walked barefoot or in cheap sandals"
why: 

For the steal:
"Gulls were the only patrols in the sky" is a nice way of introducing the theme of military impingement into the poem, which is what the poem is about, Russian impingement. It connects the sort of natural language with more militaristic or security-type language. But at the same time, he doesn't do it in too forceful a manner. Either because the word "patrols" is not as aggressive a militaristic term.

For the cut:
"walked barefoot or in cheap sandals" is not particularly evocative. It's a little clichéd, walking barefoot. Simple language sometimes can be evocative, but simple language sometimes can also fall flat

# 2026-09-02 · Tariq Al-Arabi, "Be Less Poetic"
steal: War was waiting down the road. Then 
a taxi tore past.
cut: The mother feeds her children the day’s honey 
with aﬀection and a little cunning.
why:

For the steal:
The way that this imagery works to create a sense of danger and momentum is very effective. It's a very simple idea of war waiting, but by making war wait down the road, it seems imminent and somehow like a threat, or maybe not. It's subtle.

And then the "a taxi tore past" is a very subtle thing too, because it just shows a vehicle in motion. "Tore past" implies a lot of speed, so maybe it's dangerous. I think this is a good line where it's using a trope of honey in the Arab world, and the idea of the mother giving the kids honey. It's a little trite. It's a little cliched. It's trying to use a little cunning in there to maybe undercut that, but it's not quite doing it effectively. I don't think it's really adding any value to the poem either

# 2026-09-03 · César Vallejo, "[The rage that shatters a man into children,]"
steal: The rage that shatters a man into children,
that shatters the child into identical birds,
and the bird, eventually, into tiny eggs

cut: the rage of the poor
wields one sword against two daggers

why:

For the steal:
This line does something that is, as a whole, very effective, which is that it decomposes rage. That's the first part. Explaining that "rage shadows a man into children" is very effective because it reveals a rage as essentially an immature impulse, but it also doesn't demonize it. By referring to it as "children," it almost makes it seem something simple and elemental.

But then the line takes an unexpected turn, because by shattering the child into identical birds and the birds eventually into eggs, he transfers the metaphor from one that is just about psychology and humans to something that is more fantastical, and maybe even a little bit supernatural. This introduces an element of almost the sort of magic realism into the poem

For the cut:
The rage of the poor is simple enough and that's effective, but "wields one sword against two daggers" is a very simple and basic, almost clichéd line, sword against daggers or vice versa. It's also not very clear. It's not really clear to me that one sword is outmatched by two daggers. I don't really understand what the point that the metaphor is even trying to make. He is simultaneously not a very arresting or original metaphor, nor is it very effective in terms of communicating a message

# 2026-09-05 · Robert Frost, "The Cocoon"
steal: 
And pours the elm-tree meadow full of blue


cut: 
Makes the new moon look anything but new,


why:

For the steal:
Referring to the autumn haze "pouring" into the meadow with "blue" is clever image because it connects the idea of fluid (water, blue, pour) with the natural green imagery of the meadow and elm trees. Also, elm-tree meadow as a compound image is also quite inventive and new

# 2026-09-08 · Luis Muñoz, "Folded"

That would be fine as a metaphor for something going on in your head, the different warring factions in your head. But I don't even know if that's what the poem is trying to say, because it's talking about "that might tear it," and it's almost like it's fragile rather than it's causing chaos. Something is not resonating with that word in this sentence

# 2026-09-09 · Eugenio Montale, "Noon"


Steal:
to hear from in the thick of briars
blackbirds’ blatter, the slither of adders.

Cut:
To observe through boughs the sea’s
distant shimmering of scales

Why:

For the steal:
I love the way that the use of clashing consonants and alliteration gives us a sense for the sort of gnarled, thick, overlapping, knotted experience of what the poet is seeing, hearing, and feeling. I liked the use of kind of internal rhyme within the sentence with "bladder" and "adders." I like the way that the "slither" kind of sneaks up on you, almost like a snake sneaking up on you. And overall, this sentence just has a degree of sumptuousness, but also danger, which is a great combination

For the cut:
Shimmering when it comes to the sea feels a little clichéd. Also, the way that the poem enlarges the scope here, we go from staring at the wall and very closely to far away through the bows of the tree, it happened very suddenly. It was a big step, and I think if you're going to take a step like that, you really need to nail the language. Distant shimmering of scales almost feels like mixed metaphors, scales and the shimmering. As well as language that I've seen and heard before, it's a little clichéd




https://poets.org/poem/noon-1
